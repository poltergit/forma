# FORMA — Room Personalities

**Version:** 1.0  
**Principle:** One atelier. Five distinct rooms.  
**Companion to:** [Creative DNA](./CREATIVE_DNA.md) and [Force Grammar](./FORMA_FORCE_GRAMMAR.md)

FORMA is not a collection of pages wearing the same visual skin. It is an atelier with different rooms, each designed around a different creative act. The rooms share a visual language, but each has its own composition, rhythm, and way of responding to the visitor.

A visitor should be able to recognize the room by its visual behavior before reading its title.

## Shared language, distinct expression

Every room may use Matter, Force, Trace, and Notation; the Afterform; the Neon Relic palette; Space Grotesk, Inter, and JetBrains Mono; and purposeful depth, material contrast, and motion.

Every room must decide which primitive is dominant, what the visitor is trying to do, what visual behavior explains that activity, what interaction changes the work, and what evidence remains afterward.

Do not copy a room's composition into another room simply to create consistency.

## 01 — The Manifestation / Landing

**Creative act:** Encounter the identity and understand the world of FORMA.

**Personality:** Cinematic, expressive, spatial, editorial. The landing experience is the threshold into the atelier, not a dashboard or feature directory.

**Visual grammar:** A signature specimen and living brand mark anchor the composition. Large typography behaves as part of the spatial environment. Orbital annotations and registration marks introduce the identity primitives. The page moves between distinct visual chapters rather than repeating identical sections. The Afterform reveals previous and possible states of the identity.

**Motion grammar:** Emergence, orbit, collision, transformation, reveal, and settle. Scroll choreography should make the visitor feel that they are moving through a changing material system.

**Interaction purpose:** Invite curiosity and make the identity tangible. Interactions should reveal a force or transformation, not simply decorate the pointer.

**Avoid:** Generic SaaS hero layouts, feature-card grids, constant parallax, and a logo that is only a static badge.

## 02 — The Open Field / Playground

**Creative act:** Experiment with generative rules.

**Personality:** Unruly, exploratory, playful, and immediate. It should feel like a working wall of experiments rather than a polished settings form.

**Visual grammar:** The generative stage is the primary canvas. Generator choices feel like instruments or specimen types, not interchangeable cards. Coordinates, seeds, material notes, and annotations explain the conditions behind the result. Presets are gestures or starting conditions, not finished designs. The Afterform can show displaced positions, iterations, and earlier states.

**Motion grammar:** Disturb, attract, displace, repeat, deform, and recompose. Movement must be a visible response to a parameter or pointer force.

**Interaction purpose:** Changing a parameter must visibly change the generated result. Saving a recipe preserves the rules so the visitor can return to the experiment, not just its appearance.

**Avoid:** Random particle fields, unexplained controls, endless ambient motion, and decorative generator tiles with no meaningful difference.

## 03 — The Force Room / Motion Lab

**Creative act:** Understand movement by shaping its forces and timing.

**Personality:** Kinetic, temporal, measured, and slightly theatrical. It should feel like a movement laboratory with a visual score.

**Visual grammar:** Trajectories and temporal echoes are visible around the moving object. A timeline, easing curve, or score communicates how movement unfolds. The stage gives clear attention to the moving specimen and its previous positions. Technical notation bridges artistic feeling and motion parameters.

**Motion grammar:** Pull, push, pulse, shift, stagger, recoil, and resolve. Each mode needs a different silhouette or path, not merely a different speed.

**Interaction purpose:** Duration changes how long movement takes; intensity changes how far or strongly it responds; easing changes the character of the transition. The stage and score should make those differences legible.

**Avoid:** Animations that look identical across modes, motion with no visible origin or destination, and a control panel that competes with the stage.

## 04 — The Workbench / Scene Studio

**Creative act:** Construct and direct a spatial composition.

**Personality:** Focused, tactile, precise, and quietly powerful. This is the most instrument-like room; creative freedom depends on dependable controls.

**Visual grammar:** The 3D viewport is the dominant visual plane. Object hierarchy and inspector controls frame the canvas without overpowering it. Lighting, camera, material, and render controls are treated as studio instruments. Grid lines, axes, selection outlines, and material previews provide useful notation. The Afterform may appear as a previous composition state or subtle spatial guide.

**Motion grammar:** Place, rotate, scale, illuminate, focus, and frame. Transitions should communicate spatial changes and preserve the user's sense of orientation.

**Interaction purpose:** Every tool must make a direct, understandable change to the selected object or scene. Camera and lighting adjustments should reveal material character and composition.

**Avoid:** Unnecessary cinematic movement while editing, decorative UI that reduces viewport space, and controls that obscure the specimen.

## 05 — The Specimen Index / Archive

**Creative act:** Preserve, revisit, and learn from creative history.

**Personality:** Curatorial, archival, reflective, and editorial. This room should feel like a collection of evidence rather than a generic history list.

**Visual grammar:** Saved studies are specimens with identity, date, source room, and parameters. Registration marks, serial numbers, traces, and layered imprints create provenance. The visual language privileges comparison and retrieval over spectacle. Empty states invite the first experiment and explain what the archive preserves.

**Motion grammar:** Reveal, layer, compare, rewind, and restore. Motion should feel quieter and more deliberate than the Open Field or Force Room.

**Interaction purpose:** Reopening a specimen returns the visitor to its original conditions. The archive connects the result to the process that produced it.

**Avoid:** Generic feed cards, meaningless timestamps, fake activity metrics, and visual effects that distract from retrieving work.

## Cross-room continuity

When work travels between rooms, its meaning must travel with it.

| Handoff | What must remain meaningful |
|---|---|
| Open Field → Force Room | The generator, seed, density, influence, and source context become a motion starting point |
| Open Field → Workbench | Generated geometry and its compositional identity become editable spatial matter |
| Open Field / Force Room → Specimen Index | The saved trace records its source, settings, and return path |
| Workbench → Specimen Index | A saved composition preserves the object arrangement and relevant scene settings |

A handoff should communicate the transformation: what is carried forward, what changes, and what the next room makes possible. Never make the visitor feel that their work was simply moved to another page.

## Design review questions

1. Can its personality be recognized without reading the page title?
2. Is its dominant identity primitive clear?
3. Does its composition differ meaningfully from the other rooms?
4. Does its motion grammar match the creative act?
5. Does every interaction have a clear consequence?
6. Does the room preserve the visitor's work and context?
7. Is there enough stillness for the work to be understood?
8. Does it still belong to the same FORMA atelier?

**FORMA rule: The rooms may speak in different voices. They must never tell different stories.**
